# Mage: The Sorcerers Crusade — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index. For exact quotes or precise facts, query
book_chunks.db (see README.md in this bundle for query examples)
rather than relying on the summaries below.

**This book is NOT part of the M20 (20th Anniversary) product line.**
Published in 1998, Mage: The Sorcerers Crusade is a classic-era
sourcebook designed to sync with the 1996 Mage: The Ascension Second
Edition ruleset — seventeen years before M20 existed. It's the
historical-setting prequel line (Renaissance/Age of Reason Europe,
roughly 1400s-1500s) to modern-day Mage, the same relationship
Vampire: The Dark Ages has to modern-nights Vampire. See this
library's top-level `library_index.md` for a full account of where
this book agrees with, differs from, and directly feeds into
`m20-core` — the differences are real and worth flagging, not just a
disclaimer.

**A note on page numbers:** every page number below refers to the
page's position in the source PDF file (which is also how
`book_chunks.db` tags its `page` column), *not* the book's own printed
page footer. This PDF opens with a 16-page fiction Prelude that
precedes the book's own page-numbering entirely, so **PDF page =
printed page + 19** for the rest of the book (verified at multiple
points from the credits page through the back-of-book index). If you
have the physical/PDF book open and want to find a page cited here,
add 19 to the printed number; if you're just querying `book_chunks.db`,
the numbers below already match its `page` column directly.

## Overview
Mage: The Sorcerers Crusade is a 317-page standalone core rulebook
(playable on its own, not merely a supplement) for a Renaissance-era
Mage chronicle, spanning roughly 1400-1500 CE. It uses the same
underlying Storyteller System and Nine-Sphere magick system as
classic Mage: The Ascension, narrated throughout via a continuous
in-character fiction thread (a weaver's daughter, Agnesa, apprenticed
to a Hermetic-adjacent knight-mage) that runs through every chapter,
including the rules chapters. Its central premise mirrors modern
Mage's — the tension between magickal wonder, faith, and reason,
personified by the newly-forming **Council of Nine Mystick
Traditions** on one side and the newly-forming **Order of Reason**
(Technocracy's direct predecessor) on the other — but tells that
story at its earlier, rawer, more openly supernatural stage: witch
hunts, the Black Death's aftermath, the fall of Constantinople,
early cannons and clockwork, and a magickal "Ascension War" still
being actively fought for the first time rather than inherited as
centuries-old history. This book is also historically significant to
Mage as a game: its "Future Fates" sidebar device — explicitly giving
the Storyteller three options (events will happen / might happen /
may never happen) for how a historical event resolves — is the same
mechanic M20 core adopted wholesale for its own metaplot decades later
(see `library_index.md`).

## Front Matter (pp. 1-4)
Cover art pages only. No text content.

## Prelude (pp. 5-20)
An unnumbered fiction prologue (illustrated by Greg and Tim
Hildebrandt, written by Storm Constantine — the same author credited
with M20 core's own Prelude fiction) telling the story of **Melusine**,
a young woman fleeing an arranged marriage and an inhuman "angel" that
has come to claim her, and **Sir Renaud Aquinas**, a young Hermetic
mage failing to find his Daemon (Avatar) through alchemical ritual
under the guidance of his Saracen mentor Kalid. The two storylines
converge in a snowbound forest, where Renaud recognizes the "angel"
pursuing Melusine as his own long-sought Daemon, and the two mages —
one who found his power through ritual, one who nearly died running
from it — merge in a moment of mutual Awakening. Unusually for this
book's layout, the Prelude appears *before* the Credits and Table of
Contents rather than after, as a deliberate cold open.
**Key themes:** the Awakening as something that can look like either
mystical discipline or supernatural terror depending on which side of
it you're standing on; magick as inescapable rather than chosen.

## Credits & Table of Contents (pp. 21-23)
Standard credits, legal text, and Table of Contents. Notably credits
Phil Brucato (later M20 core's own author) as concept/design lead here
too, with additional writing from Richard Dansky, Kenneth Hite, and
Deena McKinney.

## Introduction (pp. 24-32)
**Summary:** Frames the book as set during "the early Ascension War"
in Renaissance Europe (explicitly flexible across the 1400s-1500s —
a sidebar, "Historical Note," lets the Storyteller pick a narrower
window depending on which named conflicts they want to run through).
Explains the book's central conceit: an intolerant, supernatural-real
world where Magick, Faith, and Reason are framed as three competing
but overlapping paths, and where the "Council of Nine" and "Order of
Reason" are described as **newly forming, unsteady confederations**,
not yet the centuries-old institutions modern Mage takes for granted.
Introduces the **Future Fates** sidebar device in essentially the same
three-option form M20 core later uses. Closes with an extensive
two-part **Lexicon** (Common Terms, then separate Council and Order of
Reason internal terminology, pp. 28-32) that is the single richest
source of this era's distinct vocabulary — see this file's Glossary
section and `library_index.md` for how many of these terms are direct
etymological ancestors of familiar M20 terms.
**Key themes:** magick as a live, contested, unresolved conflict
rather than settled modern-day politics; deliberate historical
looseness ("this is a fantasy game, not a historical simulation," per
the book's own "Three Important Notes," p.27).
**Key terms/entities:** Future Fates mechanic (p.27), full Lexicon
(p.28-32), flexible time period guidance (p.28).

## Chapter I: The Path of Thorns (pp. 33-45)
**Summary:** The direct structural and thematic counterpart to M20
core's own Chapter One (same title concept, same position in the
book), narrated in an unnamed magus's first-person voice addressing
the reader as "magus." Covers the Awakening ("Those Whose Eyes Have
Opened"), the **Daemon** (this era's term for what M20 calls the
Avatar) and its four **Essence** natures — Castellum (Earth), Furo
(Fire), Mare (Water), and Susurro (Air/Wind), a classical-element
taxonomy notably different from M20's Dynamic/Pattern/Questing/
Primordial framework (see `library_index.md`) — and the **Forests of
Seeking**, this era's version of Seekings, with 10 crossroads/
"chambers" to pass. Lays out a four-stage magus journey — **Awareness,
Instruction, Conflict, Resolution** — up a symbolic Mountain of
Enlightenment, with **Zenith** (mastery of power short of true
transcendence) and the **Path of Gold** (the road to actual Ascension)
as distinct peaks. Covers the Nine Arts/Spheres (Connection, Entropy,
Forces, Life, Matter, Mind, Prime, Spirit, Time — the same nine
governing principles as M20, under slightly different primary names),
**Resonance**, and **the Scourge** — this era's price for reckless
magick, explicitly framed as Divine/moral punishment (virtue brings
Boons, vice brings Banes) rather than a reality-consensus mechanic;
full game rules for it appear later in Chapter VIII. Also covers
**Quintessence and Tass**, **Crays** (this era's term for Nodes,
with a named table of major European Crays and Covenants spanning
every Tradition and the Order of Reason, p.35 — including **Doissetep**
as a currently-active, embattled Hermetic Covenant, centuries before
its M20-canon destruction) and **Sanctums vs. Covenants** as shelter
types.
**Key themes:** pride and consequence, exactly as in M20 core, but
delivered through explicitly Christian/moral vocabulary (sin, Scourge,
Divine favor) rather than M20's more secular Consensus/Paradox framing;
magick as a craft with real, itemized costs.
**Key terms/entities:** the Daemon and Essence natures (p.38), Forests
of Seeking (p.38), four-stage journey/Path of Gold/Zenith (p.39), Nine
Arts (p.40), Resonance and the Scourge (p.42), Quintessence and Tass
(p.42-43), major Crays and Covenants table (p.44).

## Chapter II: The Mortal World (pp. 46-77)
**Summary:** A dense, largely real-world history/geography chapter
(Italy, Iberia/Spain-Portugal, and — beyond the sampled portion —
presumably France, England, Germany, and points further afield) that
grounds the setting's politics, trade, and religious tension in
authentic 15th-century detail (the Black Death's aftermath, the fall
of Constantinople in 1453, the rise of Venice/Florence/Milan, the
Spanish Reconquista, early printing and gunpowder), with magickal
history woven throughout. Contains **"The Convocation Period"** — a
detailed, dated historical timeline (pp. 53-57) that is this book's
single most valuable chapter for cross-referencing against M20 core's
own History section, tracing events from the **founding of the
Order of Hermes (767 CE)** and the **Convention of the White Tower
(1325)** — both matching M20 core's own dates for the same events
exactly — through the **Grand Convocation of Traditions (1457-1466)**
that founds the Council of Nine (with a full named founding roster:
Akashic Brotherhood, Ahl-i-Batin/Batini, Chakravanti/Euthanatoi,
Celestial Chorus, Dream-speakers, Order of Hermes, Seers of
Chronos/Sahajiya, Solificati, and Verbena — see `library_index.md` for
how this differs from M20's modern Nine), the **First Cabal** and its
full named membership, the **Great Betrayal (1470)** by Solificati
representative **Heylel Teomim** (the same named "Great Betrayer"
referenced in M20 core's own history), and the **Malleus Maleficarum**
(1486) triggering the long Burning Times. Also names ongoing
conflicts a Storyteller can center a chronicle on: the war against the
demonic warlord **Tezghul the Insane**, and **the Seven Thunders**
(detailed further in the Appendix). Closes noting that in 1500, the
last 12 Solificati rename themselves **the Children of Knowledge** —
directly matching the Disparate Craft of that name described in M20
core.
**Key themes:** magick as inseparable from real geopolitics, trade,
and religious violence; the instability and internal betrayal that
mark the Council's founding generation, presented as formative trauma
rather than settled backstory.
**Key terms/entities:** Order of Hermes founding 767 CE (p.53),
Convention of the White Tower 1325 (p.54-55), Grand Convocation
1457-1466 and founding Traditions roster (p.56), First Cabal and the
Great Betrayal 1470 (p.56-57), Malleus Maleficarum 1486 (p.57), Seven
Thunders and Solificati/Children of Knowledge rename 1500 (p.57).

## Chapter III: Beyond the Mists (pp. 78-89)
**Summary:** The Otherworlds chapter, narrated as a direct
second-person guided journey ("cousin, come with me across the
Mists..."). Presents a notably simpler three-layer Otherworldly
cosmology than M20 core's later, more elaborate system: **the
Penumbra** (mortal-world reflection), **Veshaya** (a middle spirit
layer — dreams of gods, men and elements — a term that does not appear
to carry forward into M20 terminology at all), and **Horizon** (which
binds them and houses the Heavens), collectively **L'umbra Haute**
(the Umbra), with **the Contrarium** as its shadow-underside housing
Purgatory and the Hells. Covers **the Periphery** (heightened magickal
awareness) and **the Gauntlet** as the threshold between worlds, using
the same core vocabulary M20 keeps for both concepts.
**Key themes:** microcosm/macrocosm ("as above, so below") as the
chapter's organizing metaphysical principle; the Otherworlds as
sacred/dangerous territory reached through ritual and risk rather
than routine travel.
**Key terms/entities:** the Three Rings and Contrarium (p.79), the
Periphery and Gauntlet (p.80).

## Chapter IV: The Characters (pp. 90-135)
**Summary:** Character creation rules (Attributes, Abilities,
Backgrounds, the Daemon/Essence, Arete, Willpower, Quintessence, the
Scourge Trait, Health) continuing the Agnesa fiction thread throughout
as connective tissue between rules sections. Largely mechanical and
period-appropriate (Merits/Flaws, equipment, and social-standing
options fit to a Renaissance setting) — not summarized field-by-field
here; query `book_chunks.db` directly for specific Trait rules.
**Key themes:** N/A (primarily mechanical chapter).

## Chapter V: The Players (pp. 136-179)
**Summary:** The chapter that actually delivers full faction identity
write-ups, parallel in function to M20 core's own Traditions/
Technocracy chapter but organized as character-creation material
rather than pure history. Opens by reframing the Council/Order
rivalry as still-forming and uncertain ("both fellowships are unsteady,
uncertain... new Traditions must replace old ones," p.117) rather than
settled opposition. Gives each of the nine founding Traditions —
including **Ahl-i-Batin** and **Solificati**, both full Council
members in this era — a rich individual profile (Philosophy, Style
and Tools, Organization, a named Primus representative, Initiation,
Daemon-type, Affinities, typical Followers/Concepts, and Stereotypes
of rival factions), sampled here in depth for the **Ahl-i-Batin**
(p.122-123: the "Subtle Ones," organized in *khanaqah* lodges under
Murid/Murshid ranks, philosophy of Unity, Mind/Correspondence/Earth
affinities). Gives the Order of Reason's Conventions equivalent
treatment, and includes a remarkable passage (p.142-143) that
explicitly narrates, in-fiction, the transformation of this era's
7-8 Order of Reason Conventions into M20's familiar 5 Technocratic
Conventions over the following centuries — Artificers becoming
Iteration X, Celestial Masters absorbing Void Seekers to become Void
Engineers, High Guild becoming the Syndicate, Hippocratic Circle
becoming the Progenitors, and the Cabal of Pure Thought's collapse
(alongside the secret Ksirafai's hidden machinations) giving rise to
the New World Order — while two new splinter groups, the **Electrodyne
Engineers** and **Difference Engineers**, defect to the Council of
Nine and become the **Sons of Ether** and **Virtual Adepts**
respectively. See `library_index.md` for why this passage is one of
the most valuable single connections between this book and M20 core.
**Key themes:** the instability and internal rivalry within both the
Council and the Order at this early stage; the seeds of every modern
Technocratic Convention and two modern Traditions sown here explicitly.
**Key terms/entities:** Ahl-i-Batin full profile (p.122-123), Order of
Reason-to-Technocracy Convention lineage passage (p.142-143).

## Chapter VI: The Storyteller (pp. 180-199)
**Summary:** Conventional Storyteller/GM advice (scene-setting,
flexibility over rigid plotting, ensemble pacing, handling difficult
players), continuing the Agnesa fiction thread through a major plot
turn (her mentor von Ulm is killed; her "brother" Bartolomeo is
revealed as an enemy and dies; Karel is revealed to have his own
hidden agenda). Comparable in scope and tone to M20 core's own
Storyteller chapter, without the extensive genre/mood/music material
M20 core includes.
**Key themes:** N/A (primarily GM-advice chapter).

## Chapter VII: Rules (pp. 202-241)
**Summary:** Pure mechanical rules chapter — dice pools, difficulties,
actions, resisted rolls, botching — continuing the Agnesa fiction
thread (she is imprisoned and interrogated over her association with
Karel and von Ulm). No narrative/setting lore beyond the fiction.
**Key themes:** N/A (mechanical chapter).

## Chapter VIII: Magick Rules (pp. 242-293)
**Summary:** The full spellcasting system — Sphere mechanics, casting
rolls — and, notably, the complete **Scourge** subsystem (pp.
231-237 in the book's own numbering): Resonance, Boons and Banes tied
explicitly to a period-appropriate menu of Virtues (Cardinal, Theological,
Pagan) and Vices (Seven Deadly Sins, Pagan Vices), each with worked
examples of what a Boon or Bane looks like in play. This is the fullest
game-mechanical expression of the moral/karmic backlash system that
M20 core's own lore describes as Paradox's historical predecessor —
worth reading directly against M20 core Ch. 10's Paradox Effect rules
for anyone interested in exactly how differently the two systems
work mechanically despite serving the same narrative role. Not
summarized Sphere-by-Sphere here; query `book_chunks.db` directly.
**Key themes:** virtue and vice as literally load-bearing game
mechanics, in a way M20's more setting-agnostic Paradox system
deliberately moved away from.
**Key terms/entities:** the Scourge/Boons and Banes system in full
(p.232-237).

## Appendix (pp. 294-308)
**Summary:** Storyteller reference material: NPC/adversary templates
(Rogue, Witch-hunter, and others, with period-appropriate gear and
Ability lists), a detailed writeup of **the Seven Thunders** (pp.
297-298 — a millenarian peasant-prophet movement, styled after
Revelation's seven thunders, convinced Judgment Day begins in 1500,
who topple lords, magi, churchmen, and philosopher-scientists alike
in their crusade), a list of real historical witch-hunting
organizations used as adversary fodder (the Spanish Inquisition, the
Society of Leopold, and several fictionalized parallels), a bestiary,
and treasure/Wonder-crafting guidance. Not summarized entry-by-entry
here.
**Key themes:** N/A (reference material).

Publisher dividers, the book's own back-of-book Index (alphabetical,

## Cross-cutting themes (whole book)
- **An Ascension War still being invented, not inherited**: unlike
  M20 core, which describes centuries of settled institutional history,
  this book's Council and Order are explicitly "unsteady, uncertain"
  (Ch. V, p.117) — the same pride-vs-consequence thesis M20 core
  states outright, but dramatized here as live, contested politics
  rather than retrospective history.
- **Faith and moral virtue as literally mechanical**: the Scourge
  system (Ch. I, Ch. VIII) ties magickal backlash directly to
  Christian/Pagan virtue and vice in a way M20's more setting-neutral
  Paradox never does — this book's cosmology is more overtly, actively
  Christian/Divine-justice-flavored than M20's.
- **Real 15th-16th century history as the load-bearing setting**
  (Ch. II): the Black Death, the fall of Constantinople, the Spanish
  Reconquista, and the witch-hunts of the Burning Times are treated
  with real historical texture, not just backdrop — mirroring M20
  core's own later commitment to weaving real-world 21st-century
  events into its metaplot (see `library_index.md`).
- **The seeds of every modern faction sown explicitly on-page**: the
  Technocracy's five Conventions, the Sons of Ether, and the Virtual
  Adepts are all given an explicit, named origin story in this book
  (Ch. V) rather than being treated as always-already-existing.
