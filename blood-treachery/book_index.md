# Blood Treachery — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index. For exact quotes or precise facts, query
book_chunks.db (see README.md in this bundle for query examples)
rather than relying on the summaries below.

**This book is part of neither the M20 line nor the classic-2nd-Edition
line already in this library.** Published in 2004 as part of White
Wolf's "Year of Revelations" line, *Blood Treachery* is a **Revised
Edition** (2000 ruleset) sourcebook — a Mage/Vampire crossover written
as a five-act interactive drama/script rather than a conventional
sourcebook, telling the story of a renewed war between the Order of
Hermes and House Tremere in the "Final Nights" (the Revised-era
lead-up to the Time of Judgment). See this library's top-level
`library_index.md` for how this book's modern-day setting connects to
both `m20-core` (same in-fiction period, later retrospective) and
`mage-sorcerers-crusade` (the *first* Massasa War this book's title
directly continues).

## Overview
*Blood Treachery* is a compact (96-page) sourcebook built almost
entirely around a single continuous five-act play (Prelude, Act One
through Four, plus a game-mechanics Appendix) rather than conventional
prose chapters. Every scene is formatted as a literal theatrical
script — Dramatis Personae, stage directions, character dialogue —
narrated with recurring interludes from eight **Oracles** (one per
Sphere: Connection, Entropy, Forces, Life, Matter, Mind, Prime, Spirit)
who comment on the action like a Greek chorus. The plot: House
Tytalus mage **Johann Kurtzweil** is drawn into an addiction to
vampire blood by his mentor **Marcus De Allegresse**, who is secretly
brokering a blood-for-information deal with the vampiric remnants of
**House Tremere** — the Order of Hermes' own thousand-year-old
"errant brethren," Hermetic wizards who turned to vampirism thirteen
generations ago. That secret becomes the spark for the Order's
Quaesitori to formally declare a second **Wizards' March** (a real
war) against House Tremere, with catastrophic, self-inflicted
consequences for the Order itself. A closing Appendix ("The Blood
Curse") gives full game-mechanical rules for ghoul mages, Sphere magic
versus vampiric Thaumaturgy, the Hermetic High Umbral spirit hierarchy,
and new Rotes built specifically for hunting or being hunted by
vampires.
**A note on tone:** the book is written with a much harder edge than
either other book in this library — frequent profanity in dialogue,
graphic violence, and a bleak "everyone loses" throughline — reflecting
its place late in the classic-era metaplot's "Final Nights"/Time of
Judgment build-up, tonally darker than either `m20-core`'s retrospective
framing or `mage-sorcerers-crusade`'s Renaissance adventure tone.

## Front Matter (pp. 1-5)
Cover, blank pages, title/credits page, and Table of Contents. No
narrative content. Credits: written by Scott Cohen and Steven Michael
DiPesa.

## Prelude: Enthralled (pp. 6-13)
**Summary:** A single continuous fiction sequence (no scene breaks)
establishing Johann Kurtzweil's vampire-blood addiction from the
inside. Opens mid-withdrawal — Kurtzweil's Avatar itself (seen as a
woman in a white dress) is craving blood alongside him — then
flashes back to the addiction's origin: his mentor **Marcus De
Allegresse** offering him a vial of vampire blood, framed as a secret,
elite House Tytalus initiation ("Ave Tytalus") that grants power
without apparent cost. The Prelude's back half jumps forward to a
botched joint Hermetic/Flambeau raid on a vampire haven in
Massachusetts (with a Verbena coven from Salem on standby for
casualties) that Kurtzweil has secretly sabotaged by tipping off the
Tremere in advance — not to protect them, but to manufacture a crisis
that will let House Tytalus seize control of the Order's response to
vampires. The raid goes catastrophically wrong regardless (Tremere
sorcery proves far more dangerous than expected), and Kurtzweil ends
the Prelude having taken a vampire prisoner, **Miguel Cordovera**, and
tasting spilled vampire blood directly from the floor.
**Key themes:** addiction narrated as literal Avatar-corruption, not
just character flaw; manufactured crises and manipulated consent as
the Order's actual internal politics; the gap between Kurtzweil's
self-justifying interior monologue ("through strife, power") and the
harm he's actually causing.
**Key terms/entities:** Johann Kurtzweil, Marcus De Allegresse
(House Tytalus), the Ars Vis (Tytalus' magical praxis), Miguel
Cordovera (a captured Ex Miscellanea-turned-vampire).

## Introduction (pp. 14-17)
**Summary:** Frames the book's premise directly: House Tremere is "a
stain, an embarrassment and the shadow that follows every ambitious
mage" for the Order of Hermes — the remnants of a thousand-year-old
Hermetic house that turned to vampirism, fought a costly war against
the Order (**the Massasa War**) centuries ago, and then faded into
mutual, uneasy silence. States outright that this book is about *how
the Order (and the Traditions generally) can renew themselves* under
pressure, not really a story about vampires at all — vampires are the
catalyst, not the subject. A **"What You Don't Know Will Kill You"**
sidebar (p.14) is an unusually direct authorial note: it tells
players that Hermetic characters should be written as genuinely
ignorant of real-world *Vampire: The Masquerade* clan/Discipline
lore, since in-fiction the Order's own vampire records were mostly
destroyed with Doissetep, scattered, or hoarded by House Tytalus.
Closes with a **"Disclaimer for Angry Gamers"** explicitly telling
Storytellers the book's plot is optional canon, not a mandate.
**Key themes:** in-world epistemic humility (mages don't know what
the players know) as an explicit design principle; renewal through
crisis as the book's stated thesis.
**Key terms/entities:** the Massasa War (historical Order-vs-Tremere
conflict), House Tremere's origin and eventual silence, "What You
Don't Know Will Kill You" sidebar (p.14).

## Act One: Wizards' March (pp. 18-29)
**Summary:** Establishes the political machinery behind the war's
declaration. Opens with a **Dramatis Personae** list keying each
named character to a Tarot Major Arcana card (mirroring the Tarot
structural device used across this library's other classic-era book).
A "Status of the Houses of Hermes" sidebar (p.19-20) surveys all ten
Hermetic Houses' state of crisis in the wake of Doissetep's fall and
Porthos Fitz-Empress's death — including House Thig's continued
independent existence and **House Solificati/the Children of
Knowledge's** recent, surprising decision to formally rejoin the
Order as its tenth House (resolving, centuries later, the schism first
described in `mage-sorcerers-crusade`). The Act's dramatic core: a
Technocratic-scale atrocity in Bangladesh, in which a single ancient
vampire reportedly kills millions and shrugs off a *nuclear strike*
before finally dying (Scene 4, p.23-24) — convinces the Order's
Quaesitori that vampires are a far greater threat than assumed.
Marcus De Allegresse manipulates the resulting panic (privately
revealed to Kurtzweil as cynical political theater, not real
conviction) into House Tytalus formally petitioning for war. The Act
ends with the Quaesitori's Grand Tribunal formally declaring the
second **Wizards' March** against House Tremere on **January 21, 2000**
(Scene 8, p.27) — with the Oracles' closing commentary warning that
"the corner is turned. There is no going back."
**Key themes:** political manufacture of consent for war; the House
system's internal fragility in the wake of Doissetep; genuine external
threat (Bangladesh) used cynically to justify an unrelated internal
power grab.
**Key terms/entities:** Status of the Houses of Hermes sidebar
(p.19-21), Bangladesh vampire incident (p.23-24), Wizards' March
declared January 21, 2000 (p.27).

## Act Two: Hidden War (pp. 30-43)
**Summary:** The war's opening battles, told as a series of short,
sharply escalating scenes rather than one continuous narrative.
Covers a doomed library-raid cabal (Adam Bell, Sophia Chevallier, and
others) that discovers vampires can carry their own mobile,
self-contained Quintessence source — their own blood — making them
far more dangerous opponents than a normal Node-bound target; Marcus
De Allegresse's own first direct taste of vampire blood, drawn from a
captured vampire Elder he tortures for research; a separate cabal
(Ingmar Strohmann and Ursula Schwartz) massacred by a vampire
assassination squad, in a scene that ends with a Bonisagus scholar
choosing not to kill a helpless vampire and instead simply walking
away ("A Double-Edged Sword" sidebar, p.36, on how little either
side actually understands about the other's history and structure);
and the destruction of the **Exeter Chantry** in London (Scene 11,
p.39-41), where a still-more-ancient **Former Tremere** councilor —
horrified by the unauthorized slaughter his own side has committed —
intervenes to save the surviving Cabal of Bright Wisdom, explicitly
invoking **Mistridge** ("Ask the peasants of Mistridge, child")
before letting the Chantry burn rather than continue the fight.
**Key themes:** both sides are more ignorant of, and more brutal
toward, each other than either realizes; individual conscience
(the sparing Bonisagus, the intervening Former Tremere) as the only
real brake on escalation; vampire blood as inherently corrupting
research material.
**Key terms/entities:** vampires as mobile Quintessence sources
(p.31), "A Double-Edged Sword" sidebar (p.36), the Former Tremere and
Mistridge reference (p.40), destruction of Exeter Chantry (p.39-41).

## Act Three: Blood Treachery (pp. 44-57)
**Summary:** The book's title Act, focused entirely on House Tytalus'
internal corruption. Marcus De Allegresse formalizes a direct
blood-for-secrecy trade with an unnamed vampire broker ("James
Diamond"), spreading vampire-blood addiction as a secret initiation
rite through multiple Tytalan cells across several cities (Quebec,
Moscow, Athens, Ontario) in a single montage sequence (Scene 2,
p.45-46) — while other Tytalans (Angelo Rossi, Vladimir Boleslav,
Maria Del Fuego) are murdered for investigating the same conspiracy.
Meanwhile Augustus Pembroke, a Tytalan who refuses the blood and
survives an assassination attempt over it, smuggles a warning and the
captured vampire Cordovera to **Horizon** for judgment by the
Council — where Cordovera, interrogated before the assembled
Traditions, breaks down describing his own soul's destruction by the
Embrace, then is unmasked as a vessel for an ancient vampire
**Ancient**'s astral projection, who monologues at length about
having personally destroyed Mistridge eight centuries earlier out of
disgust with his own pride, and warns the Order off pursuing "worse
fates than death." The Act closes on Rebecca Mitsotakai's
"we failed the world" speech (p.54) rejecting both the war and Tytalus'
corruption, and Marcus alone, sobbing that the vampire blood "is not
making us stronger — it is killing us."
**Key themes:** corruption as literally contagious and organized (not
one bad actor but a cross-continental network); the recurring
Mistridge motif as the Order's foundational trauma, invoked by both
Tremere sides as a cautionary precedent; institutional failure
diagnosed from within by a mage who played a part in causing it.
**Key terms/entities:** the House Tytalus blood-initiation conspiracy
(p.45-46), Miguel Cordovera's interrogation at Horizon (p.51-54), the
Ancient's Mistridge monologue (p.52-53), Rebecca's "we failed the
world" speech (p.54).

## Act Four: Pawns and Bishops (pp. 58-73)
**Summary:** Shifts from drama to direct Storyteller/GM essay,
addressing why the Order's magic itself is failing ("The Sleep of
Ages," p.57-58) as reality grows "exhausted" under Technocratic
Consensus pressure, and cataloging four extreme psychological
responses Tradition mages fall into under this stress — **the
Broken** (despondent, prey to Nephandi or Technocratic "rehabilitation"),
**the Webcutters** (mages who adopt Technocratic methods to fight the
Technocracy, ironically reinforcing its paradigm), **the
Transcendentalists** (mages who chase personal Ascension purely to
escape the crisis), and **the Bombs** (Flambeau-style mages
courting Quiet and Paradox in a final, reckless offensive) — with
**the Middle Path** offered as the only sustainable alternative.
Gives extensive Storytelling guidance on roleplaying House of Hermes
characters authentically (its syncretic, elitist, discipline-first
philosophy, explicitly distinguished from Technocratic authoritarianism
despite surface similarities) and on running the Wizards' March plot
itself — tone, motive, how the war escalates, how other Traditions,
the Technocracy, and ordinary mortals get pulled in, and an explicit
sidebar on inverting the whole scenario into a *Vampire: The
Masquerade*-centered chronicle instead.
**Key themes:** Tradition-wide psychological crisis as the real
subject, with the Hermetic-vampire war serving mostly as a
case-study; explicit Storyteller permission to reframe or discard the
book's own default assumptions.
**Key terms/entities:** The Sleep of Ages (p.57-58), the Four Winds
mage-response typology (p.58-59), Middle Path (p.60), "Using Blood
Treachery as a Vampire Story" sidebar (p.65).

## Appendix: The Blood Curse (pp. 74-92)
**Summary:** Full game-mechanics reference. **Ghoul Mages** (p.75-80)
covers the benefits (Strength boosts, healing, using vampire blood as
Quintessence) and severe costs (Avatar addiction that halts Arete
advancement entirely, the Blood Bond, aging reversal followed by fatal
catch-up aging if blood access is lost) of a mage drinking vampire
blood, framed throughout as a uniformly bad outcome rather than a
viable power option (see the "The Ugly Truth" and "Power Gamers Ate My
Vampire Methuselah" sidebars explicitly warning Storytellers against
letting players treat this as free power). **Magic and Vampires**
(p.80-81) gives rules for Sphere magic contesting vampiric Thaumaturgy
head-to-head, and states flatly that no mage can permanently undo the
vampiric curse itself. **The Hierarchy of the Celestial Umbrood**
(p.81-84) is a detailed Hermetic cosmology of High Umbral spirits —
the four **Elemental Archangels** (Michael/Air, Raphael/Earth,
Emmanuel/Water, Gabriel/Fire), the Elemental Courts and Tarot Arcana
beneath them, a Triple Goddess figure explicitly cross-referenced to
werewolf Gaia lore, the Zodiac spirits, and — with an explicit
in-text disclaimer that this is fiction, not real occult instruction —
an inverse "Circles of the Damned" demonic hierarchy. Closes with new
Merits/Flaws for this war (Driven, Celestial Affinity, Nephilim,
Blood-Hungry Soul, Massasa Contact, Faust's Burden) and named Rotes
built specifically for vampire-hunting or vampire-created harm
(Oathbreaker's Lash, Glorious Sword of Heaven, Oak of Sanguine Root,
Hermes' Brand), plus newer neo-Hermetic Rotes (Chaos Butterfly, The
Golden Apple) reflecting House Thig's more improvisational style.
**Key themes:** vampire blood as a mechanically real but narratively
punishing temptation, not a cheat code; the Order's spirit allies as
a genuine strategic asset in the war given spirits' natural antipathy
toward the undead.
**Key terms/entities:** ghoul mage rules and Avatar addiction (p.75-79),
Sphere magic vs. Thaumaturgy (p.80), the Four Elemental Archangels
(p.81-82), Circles of the Damned (p.84), new Merits/Flaws (p.85-87),
new vampire-hunting Rotes (p.87-91).

## Afterword from the Authors (p. 93)
Two short personal essays, one from each author (Scott Cohen and
Stephen Michael DiPesa), reflecting on belief, creative agency, and
resistance to passive consensus reality as the book's real underlying
theme — explicitly connecting the game's fiction to a call for readers
to "elevate" themselves rather than accept received truths uncritically.

## Suggested Reading (p. 94)
An annotated bibliography mixing real esoteric/literary works (James
Merrill's *The Changing Light at Sandover*, W.B. Yeats, Carl Jung's
*Psychology and Alchemy*, the *Corpus Hermeticum*) with genre fiction
(Tolkien) and practical astrology references, reflecting the same
"do your homework" research ethic Act Four recommends for players
attempting a Hermetic character.

## Back Matter (pp. 95-96)
A blank Notes page and closing/back-cover material (including a
period "What is the Onyx Path?" publishing-schedule ad). No narrative
content.

## Cross-cutting themes (whole book)
- **Corruption as organizational, not individual**: the House Tytalus
  blood conspiracy spans multiple continents and is run as a
  coordinated initiation system (Act Three), not one rogue mage's
  secret — mirroring the book's broader argument that the Order's
  crisis is systemic (Act Four's Four Winds typology) rather than a
  matter of a few bad actors.
- **Mistridge as the Order's recurring trauma-touchstone**: invoked
  independently by a Former Tremere sparing the Cabal of Bright Wisdom
  (Act Two, p.40) and by the Ancient's own confessional monologue
  (Act Three, p.52-53) — both as a moral warning against repeating
  the same cycle of pride and violence.
- **Consent, addiction, and manufactured crisis**: Kurtzweil's
  Avatar-level blood addiction (Prelude), the House Tytalus initiation
  conspiracy (Act Three), and Marcus's own cynical manipulation of the
  Bangladesh panic (Act One) are all variations on the same core
  image — power obtained by bypassing someone's (or something's) real
  consent, always narrated as corrosive rather than triumphant.
- **Explicit Storyteller permission to disagree with the book**: the
  Introduction's "Disclaimer for Angry Gamers" and Act Four's own
  admission that "many people would argue that the events of Blood
  Treachery don't make sense" are unusually candid, direct addresses
  to the reader about the book's own optional status as canon.
